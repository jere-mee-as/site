PPK 002What it is

After punk, the unrest turned inward.

Rock from the late '70s that kept punk's do-it-yourself nerve but traded the three chords for experiment, distance and art-school ideas.

Liner notes · PPK 002

The name spread through the British music press in 1978–79 for the bands that came after the Sex Pistols' first wave. Many came out of art schools; most recorded for small independent labels: Rough Trade in London, Factory in Manchester, Mute.

Joy Division (Ian Curtis, Bernard Sumner, Peter Hook, Stephen Morris) played their first gig as Warsaw in May 1977.

PPK 003Origins

1978. Punk had proved anyone could make a record.

The next wave asked what else a record could be, and found its answers in grey cities.

Manchester · Sheffield · Leeds · Glasgow · London
Akron · Cleveland · New York

Liner notes · PPK 003

June 1976: the Sex Pistols play Manchester's Lesser Free Trade Hall to a small crowd that included future members of Joy Division and The Fall. In January 1977 Buzzcocks pressed their own EP, Spiral Scratch, and showed that a band could skip the record industry.

Sheffield had Cabaret Voltaire and The Human League; Ohio had Pere Ubu and Devo; New York had no wave.

PPK 004Place

Concrete decks, cheap rent, failing on arrival.

Hulme's crescents emptied of families and filled with students, squatters and bands. A few streets away the first Factory club nights ran at the Russell Club in 1978.

Liner notes · PPK 004

Finished in 1972 to designs by Hugh Wilson and Lewis Womersley: four deck-access crescents named after Georgian architects (Adam, Barry, Nash, Kent), built where rows of terraced houses had been cleared.

Within a few years the council stopped housing families there. The flats were let cheaply, then squatted, and pulled down in 1993–94.

PPK 005Look

No band name. No title. A plot of a dead star.

Joy Division's first album, 1979: the sleeve was a radio-astronomy chart and nothing else.

Fig. 05 — drawn live from noise, one pulse per line, after Harold Craft's 1970 plot of pulsar CP 1919
Liner notes · PPK 005

The source was a plot of 80 successive radio pulses from pulsar CP 1919, made by astronomer Harold Craft from Arecibo data for his 1970 thesis and reprinted in The Cambridge Encyclopaedia of Astronomy. Bernard Sumner brought it to Peter Saville, who printed it white on black, with no band name. Factory catalogue number: FACT 10.

The lines on this page are not the original: they are drawn from noise, a new set every visit.

PPK 007Look

Mourning marble, Genoa.

The Ribaudo family tomb. Wolff's photograph of it fronted "Love Will Tear Us Apart" in 1980.

Liner notes · PPK 007

Staglieno opened in 1851 on a hillside above Genoa and became one of Europe's great monumental cemeteries: realist marble mourners, veiled women, angels.

Bernard Pierre Wolff's photographs of its tombs gave Factory two sleeves in 1980: Closer and the "Love Will Tear Us Apart" single.

Liner notes · PPK 009

Siouxsie and the Banshees formed in London in 1976 and played their first show that September at the 100 Club Punk Festival. The Scream (1978) set their cold, circling sound; by Juju (1981) her backcombed black hair and winged eyes were the look goth would copy.

The Slits' Cut and The Raincoats' first album both came out in 1979, on Island and on Rough Trade.

PPK 010Fashion

The trench coat. Then, and still.

Army surplus or charity shop, collar up, shirt buttoned to the throat. Writer Mimi Haddon's phrase for it: “Eastern Bloc chic.”

Liner notes · PPK 010

Away from studs, leather and mohawks: post-punk dressed down on purpose. Second-hand overcoats were cheap, warm in a northern winter, and made a twenty-year-old look like he had read something.

The same coat still sells every autumn, worn now as much by women as by the men in the old photographs.

Dr. Martens
PPK 011Fashion

The uniform is still in production.

Black, navy, grey. One collar, buttoned. Trousers straight and a little long. Nothing that shines. The shops that sell it now are the ones the scene shopped at then.

Liner notes · PPK 011

Margaret Howell has made plain shirts and coats in London since the early 1970s. Fred Perry's laurel shirt went from tennis to mods, skinheads and 2 Tone. The Dr. Martens 1460 boot dates from 1960.

For a sharper, darker version of the same idea, Rick Owens.

PPK 012Fashion

Monochrome. Androgynous. Anti-rockstar.

Short hair, a drab coat, trousers that don't quite fit. Dressed less like a star than a junior clerk: a deliberate non-image.

PPK 014Sound

Facing Europe, not America.

Krautrock's motorik pulse, Kraftwerk's synthesizers, dub's empty space.

Wire · The Fall · Gang of Four · The Pop Group · Cabaret Voltaire · Pere Ubu · Talking Heads · Young Marble Giants

Fig. 14 — 400 Blows at the Casbah
Liner notes · PPK 014

The steady motorik beat came from Can and Neu!, and the machines from Kraftwerk. From dub reggae came echo and space: Dennis Bovell produced The Slits' Cut, and Adrian Sherwood's On-U Sound worked with The Pop Group's circle.

By 1980 Talking Heads' Remain in Light was borrowing from Fela Kuti.

PPK 015After

Then came
the eyeliner.

Siouxsie · Robert Smith · Bauhaus · the Batcave, 1982

Liner notes · PPK 015

Siouxsie Sioux and Robert Smith added dark lipstick, teased hair and heavy eyeliner. Bauhaus released "Bela Lugosi's Dead" in 1979. The Batcave club opened in Soho in 1982, the same year as The Cure's Pornography.

Goth grew out of post-punk, and the two still share a wardrobe.

PPK 016Now

It never quite ended.

Every few years a new band picks up the dry drums and the long coat.

Interpol · Savages · Shame · Dry Cleaning · Fontaines D.C.

Liner notes · PPK 016

Interpol, Editors and Franz Ferdinand brought the sound back in the 2000s. Savages (with Jehnny Beth singing), Shame, Dry Cleaning (with Florence Shaw's spoken vocals) and Fontaines D.C. have carried it since.

PPK 017Idea

Experiment over energy.

Refuse the formula punk had already hardened into. Refuse the rock-star pose. Make something that can't be sold back to you as a costume.
